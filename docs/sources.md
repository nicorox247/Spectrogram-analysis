# Sources

The rubric requires at least 5 scholarly sources. The list below covers each major argument in the video. Aim to actually read meaningful chunks of these and deploy specific ideas — your professor can tell when sources are decorative versus integrated.

## Required Minimum: 5 Scholarly Sources

### 1. Sethares, William A. *Tuning, Timbre, Spectrum, Scale*. 2nd ed. Springer, 2005.
**Use for:** General foundation on the math/physics of timbre, the harmonic series, and the relationship between spectrum and perceived sound. Cite in the spectrogram explainer section and whenever you make claims about harmonics.

### 2. Sterne, Jonathan. *The Audible Past: Cultural Origins of Sound Reproduction*. Duke University Press, 2003.
**Use for:** The medium-as-message argument. Sterne's central claim — that sound reproduction technology shapes what counts as music, not just how we listen to it — is the theoretical backbone of your whole project. Cite in the rock section especially.

### 3. Veal, Michael E. *Dub: Soundscapes and Shattered Songs in Jamaican Reggae*. Wesleyan University Press, 2007.
**Use for:** The dub section. This is the standard scholarly text on dub aesthetics, sound system culture, and the technical practices of producers like King Tubby and Lee Perry. Essential.

### 4. Henriques, Julian. *Sonic Bodies: Reggae Sound Systems, Performance Techniques, and Ways of Knowing*. Continuum, 2011.
**Use for:** The embodied/physical experience of sub-bass. Henriques argues that sound system bass is felt through the body, not just heard, which is exactly the argument you need to make about why sub-bass matters aesthetically. Cite in dub section and possibly hip-hop section.

### 5. Schloss, Joseph G. *Making Beats: The Art of Sample-Based Hip-Hop*. Wesleyan University Press, 2004.
**Use for:** Hip-hop production aesthetics, the role of the drum machine, and the genre's relationship to its technological tools. Cite in the hip-hop section.

## Strong Optional Additions

### 6. Pinch, Trevor and Frank Trocco. *Analog Days: The Invention and Impact of the Moog Synthesizer*. Harvard University Press, 2002.
**Use for:** The history of electronic sound generation. Useful if you want to ground the "synthesizers opened new spectral territory" claim with a specific technological history.

### 7. Sterne, Jonathan. *MP3: The Meaning of a Format*. Duke University Press, 2012.
**Use for:** Further depth on how compression and format shape what music is. Less directly relevant than *The Audible Past*, but Sterne is excellent and citing two of his books shows engagement.

### 8. Marshall, Wayne. Various articles on drum machines and global hip-hop. (Wayanne Marshall has written extensively on the 808 specifically; search Google Scholar.)
**Use for:** The 808 as a specific cultural and technological object. His blog "Wayne and Wax" also has substantive technical/musicological writing if you need accessible references.

### 9. Schaefer, John. *New Sounds: A Listener's Guide to New Music*. Harper & Row, 1987.
**Use for:** Slightly older but useful for situating electronic and experimental music within 20th-century currents.

### 10. Demers, Joanna. *Listening Through the Noise: The Aesthetics of Experimental Electronic Music*. Oxford University Press, 2010.
**Use for:** Theoretical framing for noise, texture, and non-pitched sound as musical content.

## How to Find These

- **Columbia Libraries** has all of the books listed above either physically or as ebooks. Use CLIO to search.
- **JSTOR** for journal articles that cite or build on these texts.
- **Google Scholar** for finding additional academic articles on specific subtopics (e.g., "808 drum machine cultural history").
- The 808 documentary (2015, dir. Alexander Dunn) is not scholarly but features interviews with many of the relevant figures and can be cited as a primary source.

## Citation Style

Pick one and be consistent. The rubric says MLA, APA, or Chicago. For a music humanities project, **Chicago Notes-Bibliography** is the most common in musicology and looks the most "academic" for citations of books and recordings. But MLA is fine if you're more comfortable.

## Reading Strategy (Given Time Constraints)

You don't need to read these cover to cover by May 13. For each, target the chapters or sections directly relevant to your sections of the video:

- **Sethares:** Chapters 1-3 (the basics of spectrum and harmonic series). Skim later chapters.
- **Sterne, Audible Past:** Introduction and chapter on the social construction of sound reproduction.
- **Veal:** Chapters on the technical practices of dub production. Skim historical chapters.
- **Henriques:** Introduction and the chapters on bass and embodiment.
- **Schloss:** Chapters on the drum machine and sample-based production aesthetics.

This is enough to cite specific arguments with page numbers, which is what makes citations look like real engagement.

## Track Sources to Include

Don't forget to cite the recordings themselves in your bibliography. Format depends on your citation style, but include:
- Performer/composer
- Album/track name
- Label
- Year
- Recording format consulted (CD, FLAC, etc.)
