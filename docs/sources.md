# Sources

The rubric requires at least 5 scholarly sources. The list below is organized by how central each source is to the new thesis. Aim to read meaningful chunks and deploy specific ideas — your professor can tell when sources are decorative versus integrated.

## Core Sources (cite these directly in the video)

### 1. Sethares, William A. *Tuning, Timbre, Spectrum, Scale*. 2nd ed. Springer, 2005.
**Use for:** The mathematical/physical foundation of timbre and the harmonic series. Central to Sections 2 and 3 — the spectrogram explainer and the acoustic instrument analysis. Cite whenever you make claims about harmonics, spectral fingerprints, or the relationship between waveform and perceived sound.
**Read:** Chapters 1–3 (spectrum, timbre, harmonics basics). Skim later chapters.

### 2. Pinch, Trevor and Frank Trocco. *Analog Days: The Invention and Impact of the Moog Synthesizer*. Harvard University Press, 2002.
**Use for:** The history of the Moog and early synthesis — now the central technological case study in Section 4. Grounds the claim that synthesis is sound design rather than sound recording.
**Read:** The chapters covering Moog's development and early adoption. The cultural reception chapters are also useful for Section 6.

### 3. Chowning, John. "The Synthesis of Complex Audio Spectra by Means of Frequency Modulation." *Journal of the Audio Engineering Society* 21, no. 7 (1973): 526–534.
**Use for:** The FM synthesis claim in Section 4c — the DX7 and Yamaha's mainstream breakthrough. This is the original paper that FM synthesis is based on, and it's explicitly Fourier mathematics. Citing the primary source here is a strong move.
**Read:** The full paper is short (~8 pages). Read it.

### 4. Heideman, Michael T., Don H. Johnson, and C. Sidney Burrus. "Gauss and the History of the Fast Fourier Transform." *IEEE ASSP Magazine* 1, no. 4 (1984): 14–21.
**Use for:** The FFT history claim in Section 4a — Cooley and Tukey's 1965 algorithm as the pivot moment. Grounding this claim with a scholarly source rather than a Wikipedia summary matters.
**Read:** First half of the paper covers the historical narrative you need.

### 5. Schloss, Joseph G. *Making Beats: The Art of Sample-Based Hip-Hop*. Wesleyan University Press, 2004.
**Use for:** Sampling and hip-hop production aesthetics in Section 5. Schloss is the standard scholarly account of how producers think about samples, loops, and the drum machine as creative tools.
**Read:** Chapters on the drum machine, sampling practice, and production aesthetics.

### 6. Sterne, Jonathan. *The Audible Past: Cultural Origins of Sound Reproduction*. Duke University Press, 2003.
**Use for:** The cultural payoff argument in Section 6 — that sound reproduction technology reshapes what music is, not just how we listen to it. Still relevant even after the pivot.
**Read:** Introduction and the chapter on the social construction of listening.

## Optional / Supporting Sources

### 7. Sterne, Jonathan. *MP3: The Meaning of a Format*. Duke University Press, 2012.
**Use for:** If you want to extend the Fourier argument to compression — MP3 encoding is explicitly based on psychoacoustic Fourier analysis. Good for a brief mention in Section 5 or 6.

### 8. Roads, Curtis. *The Computer Music Tutorial*. MIT Press, 1996.
**Use for:** Technical depth on synthesis and sampling if you need to go deeper on any claim in Sections 4–5. Dense reference book — use the index to find relevant sections.

### 9. Demers, Joanna. *Listening Through the Noise: The Aesthetics of Experimental Electronic Music*. Oxford University Press, 2010.
**Use for:** Theoretical framing for electronic timbre as aesthetic content rather than deficiency. Useful background for Section 4d.

## How to Find These

- **Columbia Libraries (CLIO)** — all books above are held physically or as ebooks
- **JSTOR / IEEE Xplore** — for the Chowning and Heideman journal articles specifically
- **Google Scholar** — for additional articles citing Chowning's FM synthesis paper

## Citation Style

**Chicago Notes-Bibliography** is standard in musicology and handles the mix of books, journal articles, and recordings cleanly. Use it unless you're already comfortable with MLA.
